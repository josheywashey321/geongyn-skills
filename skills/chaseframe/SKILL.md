# chaseframe — behavioral influence for marketing work

Chase Hughes' behavior and influence frameworks, applied to copy, layout, campaigns, and live selling.

## The core principle

**FATE: Focus → Authority → Tribe → Emotion.** Four levers, in the order they must be pulled. Hughes calls them the four ways to influence a mammal, and he maps them onto advertising himself:

> "It's also how an infomercial gets you to buy something. They grab your focus, they show you here's a million people who've already bought it… now we get emotion, and they show it *visually*."

**It is a ladder, not a menu.** Each rung is load-bearing for the next:

| Missing | What the reader does |
|---|---|
| Focus | Never enters |
| Authority | Reads it, doesn't credit it |
| Tribe | Believes you, decides it's for someone else |
| Emotion | Agrees completely, does nothing |

Most marketing that "looks fine but doesn't convert" has every element present in the wrong order, or one rung missing. **Find the broken rung before rewriting anything.**

## Before writing a single line

Copy cannot read anyone. A live read adapts to a person; a page has no subject to observe. So profile the **segment** and commit to it:

1. **Dominant need** — significance, approval, acceptance, intelligence, pity, or power
2. **Paired fear** — the sharper of the two levers
3. **Decision filter** — social, conformity, investment, or necessity

State these three explicitly before producing anything. **Need tells you what to say; filter tells you how to justify it.**

Copy written to three segments at once averages three needs into a voice that triggers none. If the segments truly differ, that is three pages — not one page with three columns.

## The two rules

**1. Commit to one segment.** Refusing to choose is the most common failure this skill prevents.

**2. The frameworks decide sequence, framing and emphasis — never whether a claim is true.** No invented statistics, no manufactured scarcity, no fabricated proof, no "2 spots left." This is Geongyn's standing verified-claims rule, and it is also *why* the techniques work: a real finding survives being pushed on, a manufactured one collapses the first time a buyer checks. Applied to true claims this is very good marketing. The persuasion lives in the order and the framing; the claims are just true.

## Common mistakes

Observed in unaided output on a real brief — these are the specific failures this skill exists to correct.

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| **Conventional headline.** Honors every convention a skimmer expects, so it interrupts nothing | Break autopilot. Swap test: put a competitor's name on it — if it still works, it broke nothing |
| **Authority as a logo bar** | Logos are tribe-by-association at best. Authority is precision, methodology, named humans, and one honest limitation |
| **Hedging across every audience** ("Who we serve: contractors, builders, architects") | Commit to one need and one filter |
| **Proof before attention is earned** | A trust bar at position two is spent unregistered. Authority before proof, always |
| **No emotional rung** | The reader agrees with every word and does nothing. Cost of the status quo, concrete and true |
| **Sections ordered by topic** | Topic order optimizes for finding information. Order by what state the reader must be in to accept the next section |

## Quick diagnostic

When something is not converting, identify the rung before touching the copy:

- Nobody engages → **Focus**
- Traffic, no depth → **Authority**
- Engaged, never shortlisted → **Tribe**
- Shortlisted, then stalls forever → **Emotion**
- Stall treated as a pricing problem → almost always **Emotion**; discounting a stall removes the last reason to value the thing
