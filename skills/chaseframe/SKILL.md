## The core principle

**FATE: Focus → Authority → Tribe → Emotion.** Four levers, in the order they must be pulled — Hughes says himself they are "listed in order of importance", and maps them onto advertising unprompted:

> "It's also how an infomercial gets you to buy something. They grab your focus, they show you here's a million people who've already bought it… now we get emotion, and they show it *visually*."

**It is a ladder, not a menu.** Each rung is load-bearing for the next:

| Missing | What the reader does |
|---|---|
| Focus | Never enters |
| Authority | Reads it, doesn't credit it |
| Tribe | Believes you, decides it's for someone else |
| Emotion | Agrees completely, does nothing |

Most marketing that "looks fine but doesn't convert" has every element present in the wrong order, or one rung missing. **Find the broken rung before rewriting anything.**

Two things his recent material adds to the ladder: **focus comes from novelty first** — open with a subtle surprise and put the first authority cue in the same opening window; and **action comes from identity** — people move when the step confirms who they believe they are.

## Before writing a single line

Copy cannot read anyone. A live read adapts to a person; a page or video has no subject to observe. So profile the **segment** and commit to it:

1. **Dominant need** — significance, approval, acceptance, intelligence, pity, or strength/power (his 2018 map)
2. **Paired fear** — the sharper of the two levers
3. **Decision style** — deviance, novelty, social, conformity, investment, or necessity
4. **Identity line** — who they want to be, about 10% ahead of how they see themselves now

State these four explicitly before producing anything. **Need tells you what to say; decision style tells you how to justify it; identity tells you what the action means to them.**

Copy written to three segments at once averages three needs into a voice that triggers none. If the segments truly differ, that is three pages — not one page with three columns.

## The three rules

**1. Commit to one segment.** Refusing to choose is the most common failure this skill prevents.

**2. The frameworks decide sequence, framing and emphasis — never whether a claim is true.** No invented statistics, no manufactured scarcity, no fabricated proof, no "2 spots left", **and no Hughes statistic** — he misstates studies (see evidence.md). This is Geongyn's standing verified-claims rule, and it is also *why* the techniques work: a real finding survives being pushed on, a manufactured one collapses the first time a buyer checks. The persuasion lives in the order and the framing; the claims are just true.

**3. Who comes before what.** Hughes: "there's no magic words." The structures amplify a composed, credible, genuinely perceptive speaker and cannot replace one. Run his ethics test on anything identity-level: *if they could see inside your head and would still agree, it's ethical.*

## Common mistakes

Observed in unaided output on real briefs — these are the specific failures this skill exists to correct.

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| **Conventional headline or hook.** Honors every convention a skimmer expects, so it interrupts nothing | Break autopilot with a subtle, true surprise. Swap test: put a competitor's name on it — if it still works, it broke nothing |
| **Opening on a bare question** ("Want more leads?") | Statement first — alignment, resonance or a concession — then the question |
| **Persuasive lines aimed at "you"** | Dissociate: "owners who…", "most shops…". Observations about the world aren't resisted |
| **Spelling out the conclusion** | Two true facts side by side, then stop. Ideas people reach themselves aren't resisted |
| **Authority as a logo bar**, or arriving late in a video | Authority is precision, method, composure and one honest limitation — placed in the opening window |
| **Hedging across every audience** ("Who we serve: contractors, builders, architects") | Commit to one need, one decision style, one identity |
| **Fake urgency** (timers, "2 spots left") | Identity beats scarcity: "this is for owners who…", backed by a real qualifier |
| **Over-explaining** | The person who explains the most has the least power. One reason, said once |
| **CTA with no expectancy** ("Book a call") | Say exactly what happens next and what they leave with |
| **No emotional rung** | The reader agrees and does nothing. Cost of the status quo, concrete and true |
| **Sections ordered by topic** | Order by what state the reader must be in to accept the next section |
| **Citing Hughes to a client or buyer** | Never. His credentials are publicly disputed; it costs you the Authority rung |

## Quick diagnostic

When something is not converting, identify the rung before touching the copy:

- Nobody engages → **Focus** (no novelty, or a bare-question opener)
- Views but no depth → **Authority** (no credibility cue in the opening window)
- Engaged, never shortlisted → **Tribe** (they think it's for someone else — the identity line is missing)
- Shortlisted, then stalls forever → **Emotion**
- Yes, then nothing → **compliance, not alignment** — check whether energy rose or dropped after the yes
- Stall treated as a pricing problem → almost always **Emotion**; discounting a stall removes the last reason to value the thing
