# The Hughes canon — sourced

Chase Hughes' frameworks in plain terms, with provenance attached. Every entry is tagged so you know how much weight it carries. Where sources disagree, that is stated rather than resolved by picking the most confident-sounding version.

**Provenance tags**

| Tag | Means |
|---|---|
| `[primary]` | Hughes' own words — recorded interview, his own site, his own course material |
| `[secondary]` | Third-party book summaries and write-ups. Directionally reliable, details drift |
| `[contested]` | Sources actively disagree. Do not assert a single version |

Descriptions here are written from research, not reproduced from his books. For the material itself: *The Ellipsis Manual*, *Six-Minute X-Ray*, *The Behavior Ops Manual*.

## FATE — the influence sequence `[primary]`

**Focus → Authority → Tribe → Emotion.** The four levers, in the order they must be pulled.

Hughes, on the Diary of a CEO podcast:

> "The four ways to manipulate or influence a mammal — and this is human, dog, you name it — is through Focus, Authority, Tribe and Emotion."

He maps it onto advertising himself, unprompted, in the same interview:

> "It's also how an infomercial gets you to buy something. They grab your focus, they show you here's a million people who've already bought it, they all have these five-star reviews, everybody in your neighborhood's doing it… they're getting success and all of their friends love them because of that success. Now we get emotion, and they show it *visually*."

Asked where authority sits in a soft-drink ad, he answers: on-screen presence, brand recognition, or a celebrity — "and this is coming from a tribal leader, as if our ancestors didn't listen to a tribal leader, you don't live very long."

**FATE is a ladder, not a menu.** Each rung is load-bearing for the next. This is the most useful single idea in the framework for marketing work:

| Missing rung | What the audience does |
|---|---|
| No Focus | Nothing else happens. They never entered. |
| Focus, no Authority | They read it and don't credit it. You are noise with good grammar. |
| Focus + Authority, no Tribe | They believe you, and conclude it is for someone else. |
| Focus + Authority + Tribe, no Emotion | They agree, they nod, they don't move. Agreement is not action. |

Marketing that "looks fine but doesn't convert" usually has all four elements present in the wrong order, or one rung missing entirely. Diagnosing the broken rung is faster than rewriting everything.

## FIC — the attention ladder inside Focus `[primary]`

**Focus → Interest → Curiosity**, developed in that sequence. The sub-structure of FATE's first rung.

Hughes, Science of Success podcast, 2017:

> "FIC is focus, interest and curiosity. The first part of that is focus, and the easiest way to establish or get someone to start focusing on you is to have authority."

Note the loop: authority is also the fastest route to focus. The top two rungs feed each other. In practice, an authority signal placed above the fold does double duty — it buys attention *and* earns credit for everything after it.

His sequence: authority activates attention → something unusual creates interest → interest is where curiosity can be opened. Curiosity is the state that pulls a reader forward.

## Autopilot and the RAS `[primary]`

The brain runs role-specific scripts to conserve energy. Hughes' example: a store employee greeting her hundredth customer runs the same mental script as the first. He calls this **autopilot mode**.

The **Reticular Activating System** is the gatekeeper. It does not wake for the expected. It wakes for two things: deviation from pattern, and social value or threat.

> "This RAS is consistently looking for threats, things that are threatening to you or things that are socially valuable. If you're in a doctor's office, all of your attention is going to go to the doctor… If George Clooney walks in, all of your attention is going to go to George Clooney."

**Three things shut off autopilot** `[secondary, consistent with primary]`:

1. **Unusual speech** — unexpected volume, tone, word choice, confusion, accent
2. **Unusual behavior** — atypical gesture, gait, touch, appearance
3. **Authoritative presence** — status that commands automatic attention

His live example of breaking a script: asking a barista which direction northeast is. They have never been asked; they go internal; they drop out of employee mode for a few seconds.

**The marketing translation, and it is the important one: scrolling is autopilot.** A visitor arrives running a script — *marketing page, skim, leave*. Every convention the page honors keeps that script running. Deviation is the only thing that interrupts it. This is why the most polished, most conventional page in a category can also be the most ignorable one.

## Authority — five pillars and the agentic shift `[primary]`

> "There are five basic qualities that dictate authority, and one of them is interchangeable… dominance, discipline, leadership, gratitude and fun, or just having a sense of adventure."

**Dominance does not mean domineering** — he is explicit: "You can be dominant and still be completely supportive and nice to everyone around you." The one substitutable pillar is dominance, replaceable by **ambition** (his example: a starving artist opening a gallery).

`[contested — minor]` One book summary lists the pillars as Control, Discipline, Leadership, Gratitude, Enjoyment. Same shape, different first and last terms. The spoken version above is primary; treat the variance as paraphrase drift, not two systems.

**The agentic shift** — Hughes considers this the most overlooked finding in the influence literature:

> "What happens when we get exposed to authority, we go through what Dr. Milgram called an agentic shift. While this shift is taking place, our brain actually shifts responsibility for our own actions onto the person that's telling us to do something… A person makes a shift to where they no longer feel responsible for their actions just in the presence of someone they think might be an authority figure."

For marketing: authority does not merely make a claim more believable. It **lowers the felt risk of acting on the claim**, because responsibility for the outcome transfers to the authority. This is why a strong authority block quietly dissolves objections you never explicitly answered.

His entry point for building authority — notably not a technique:

> "Start to express genuine interest in other people and make them feel interesting, not interested… remember the phrase leadership through support."

## The six social needs and their paired fears `[primary in 6MX, corroborated across 3 independent sources]`

Everyone runs **two** of the six. Each need has a mirror-image fear, and the fear is the sharper of the two levers.

| Need | Paired fear | How it shows | The lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Significance** — to feel important | Being dismissed, mocked, treated as insignificant | Status markers, mentions accomplishments and titles | Recognition; being treated as consequential |
| **Approval** — to be validated | Rejection, disapproval, disdain | People-pleasing, checks for reaction, softens positions | Explicit reassurance they are doing it right |
| **Acceptance** — to belong | Criticism, alienation, being cast out | "We" language, group membership, consensus framing | In-group signals; everyone like them is already here |
| **Intelligence** — to be seen as smart | Being seen as dumb, ignorant, fooled | Technical vocabulary, corrects details, resists being sold to | Respect their expertise; give data, not pitch |
| **Pity** — to have their difficulty seen | Being ignored, disbelieved, minimized | Recounts hardship, emphasizes what they have endured | Acknowledge the difficulty as real before offering a fix |
| **Power** — to be seen as strong | Being disrespected, challenged, seen as weak | Volume, leadership stories, control of the frame | Control and optionality; never a position that reads as submission |

`[secondary]` One framing adds childhood-deficit origins for each need (the Status Seeker lacked acknowledgment, the Belonger lacked inclusion). Useful as a memory aid, not load-bearing.

**Fear beats need.** Hughes' own illustration: with an intelligence-need buyer you do not praise their intelligence — you tell a story about someone who made an unintelligent choice by not buying, and let them decline to be that person. This is the mechanism underneath "loss aversion outperforms gain framing," made specific to the individual.

## The four decision filters `[secondary]`

How a person converts information into a yes. Also called decision-making style.

| Filter | The question they are actually asking | Frame that lands |
|---|---|---|
| **Social** | What will others think of me for this? | Who else chose it, and what it says about them |
| **Conformity** | What is the standard? What are the rules? | Code, spec, industry standard, the normal thing to do |
| **Investment** | What does this cost and return over time? | Lifecycle math, durability, total cost of ownership |
| **Necessity** | Does this simply have to be done? | The forcing function, the deadline, the failure prevented |

Need and filter are independent axes. **Need tells you what to say; filter tells you how to justify it.** The same true claim gets framed four different ways depending on filter — and a claim aimed at the wrong filter reads as irrelevant rather than false, which is worse, because irrelevant gets no rebuttal and no second look.

## The Human Needs Map `[contested]`

**Sources disagree on the count and do not reconcile.**

- **Six** social needs in *Six-Minute X-Ray* — best corroborated, the table above
- **Seven** in Hughes' own 2017 description of the Ellipsis needs map: "There's only seven needs on this needs map. If you can identify these needs, it will also identify fears and insecurities for that person." `[primary]`
- **Seventeen** psychological needs per one summary of *The Ellipsis Manual*, described as memorized by visualizing rooms in a house

These are plausibly different tools from different books rather than a contradiction — but no source says so. **Use the six. Never cite a count as fact.** "Hughes identifies six social needs" is defensible; "the needs map has seventeen needs" is a coin flip.

## The Behavioral Table of Elements (BToE) `[secondary]`

A periodic-table-style chart of 120+ human behaviors, built for live observation.

- Each behavior carries a **deception rating from 1.0 to 4.0**
- **Vertical axis:** head to feet
- **Horizontal axis:** low-stress to high-deception
- Each cell reportedly holds ~14 data points — confirming gestures, amplifying gestures, cultural prevalence, and when the behavior is most diagnostic relative to a statement (before, during, after)

Requires a live subject and sustained practice. See [realtime.md](realtime.md).

## The Social Stability Scale `[secondary]`

Three dimensions, each scored 1–3:

1. **Locus of control** — external to internal
2. **Following behavior** — absorbs others' emotions vs. emotionally insulated
3. **Esteem** — seeks validation vs. self-assured

Combined with needs identification it yields a compact profile string, e.g. *"Approval, Power, 1, 3, 2."*

## The Ellipsis Progression `[secondary]`

The full arc of the system in *The Ellipsis Manual*.

**Operator phases:** approach → profiling → authority development → linguistic work → trance development → activation

**Subject's corresponding arc:** doubt → trust → hyper-focus → unconscious agreement

**Attentional captivity** is the first operational goal — the state where a subject is absorbed enough that competing stimuli fade, analogous to forgetting you are in a theatre. Everything deeper is built on it.

## Behavioral clusters and the four channels `[secondary]`

Every interaction broadcasts on four channels at once:

1. **Verbal** — the words (least reliable)
2. **Paraverbal** — tone, pace, pitch, volume (leaks stress before the body does)
3. **Kinesic** — posture, gesture, movement (largely involuntary)
4. **Micro-expression** — facial flashes lasting fractions of a second (hardest to fake)

**A single signal means nothing.** A cluster — several channels shifting at once, at a specific moment — is the unit of meaning. The highest-information event is **contradiction between channels**: calm words delivered at double the normal speaking pace is the moment worth noticing.
