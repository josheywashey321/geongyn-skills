# Copywriting

## The step that everything else depends on

Copy cannot read anyone. 6MX is a live, one-on-one read: baseline the person, watch deviations, adapt in real time. A page has no subject to observe and no ability to adjust.

**So you profile the segment, not the person** — and you commit. Before writing a word, fix three things:

1. **Dominant need** (of the six, 2018 map) for this buyer type
2. **Paired fear** — which is the sharper lever
3. **Decision style** (of six) — how they justify a yes
4. **Identity line** — who they want to be, about 10% ahead of how they see themselves now (A-B8L)

Write them down. Every line answers to them.

**Committing is the whole technique.** A page written to "contractors, builders and architects" equally is written to no one — it averages three needs into a voice that triggers none. If the segments genuinely differ, that is not one page with three columns, it is three pages, or one page and two lanes off it. The instinct to serve everyone in one hero is the most common failure this skill exists to prevent.

### Common segment reads

Starting points, not conclusions — verify against real customer language.

| Buyer | Likely need | Paired fear | Filter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spec-writer / architect | Intelligence | Being seen as dumb — specifying wrong | Conformity |
| Contractor / builder | Strength/Power or Intelligence | Disrespect; being sold to | Investment / Necessity |
| Owner-operator (trades) | Significance | Being dismissed as small-time | Investment |
| Procurement | Intelligence | Blamed for a bad call | Conformity |
| Homeowner, high-ticket | Approval or Acceptance | Rejection; making a fool of themselves | Social |
| Founder / exec buyer | Significance or Strength/Power | Irrelevance; loss of control | Investment |

## Need → language

What each need requires from the copy, and what kills it.

| Need | Write | Never |
|---|---|---|
| **Significance** | Treat them as consequential. Their name, their project, their standards. "For shops that spec their own details." | Lump them in with everyone. "Businesses of all sizes." |
| **Approval** | Reassure the choice is correct and normal. Explicit permission. "You're not behind — most shops are here." | Imply they should have acted sooner. |
| **Acceptance** | In-group markers. Their vocabulary, their region, their trade. "You already know what a bad clip line costs you." | Outsider framing, generic corporate register. |
| **Intelligence** | Give data and let them conclude. Specs, numbers, tradeoffs, the honest limitation. | Hype, adjectives, anything that reads as being sold to. This need punishes salesmanship hardest. |
| **Pity** | Name the difficulty as real before the fix. "Nobody tells you the spec sheet is the bottleneck." | Skip to the solution. It reads as not having listened. |
| **Strength/Power** | Give control and options. They decide, you supply. "Pull the numbers yourself." | Anything that reads as you steering. Urgency pressure inverts on this need. |

## Decision style → reframe

Same true claim, six framings. Pick one and hold it.

Claim: *published span tables and CAD files on the site.*

| Filter | Framing |
|---|---|
| **Social** | "The shops other builders call first are the ones whose numbers you can pull up on site." |
| **Conformity** | "Span and load data published to the same standard your inspector expects to see." |
| **Investment** | "Twenty minutes per takeoff, every takeoff, for the life of the account." |
| **Necessity** | "You cannot submit the drawing without the load table. Right now that's a phone call and a wait." |
| **Deviance** | "Most suppliers still make you call for numbers. We put them on the page." |
| **Novelty** | "Span tables your phone can read on site — new in this category." *(only if it is)* |

Wrong decision style is worse than a weak claim. A weak claim gets argued with; an irrelevant one gets skipped, and skipped means it never entered.

## Sentence-level tools from the 2026 research

Detail and sources in [techniques.md](techniques.md) and [identity.md](identity.md).

- **Statement before question.** Never open a section, an email or a hook with a bare question; lead with an alignment, resonance or concession statement (A-VD1).
- **Dissociate.** Aim persuasive lines at "owners who…" or "most shops", not "you" (A-B8L 12:24).
- **Two Legos.** Two true facts side by side, no "therefore" (D-DOAC-2 1:04).
- **Name the category early, and make it boring.** "A risk problem", not "a growth opportunity" (A-PYf).
- **Explain once.** The person who explains the most has the least power; justification invites negotiation (A; B).
- **Comparison with "yet".** "It names two suppliers. Not you. Yet." keeps the gap closable (C-SRS 1:08).
- **Borrow a trusted format.** An audit report, a spec sheet or an inspection checklist makes the reader run that format's "act on this" script (B).
- **Identity beats scarcity.** "This is for shops that…" — with a real qualifier — instead of a deadline (B-1Ua).

## FATE at the sentence level

The ladder runs the page, and it also runs a section, an email, and a single paragraph.

### Focus — the headline is an autopilot break

The reader arrives running *marketing page, skim, leave*. A conventional headline is fully compatible with that script and does not interrupt it.

Patterns that break the script, in rough order of strength:

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **Specific checkable number where a vague claim belongs** | "Your span table is on page 14 of a PDF. Your buyer is on a roof." |
| **The thing the category won't say** | "Most of our competitors' spec sheets are unreadable. So were ours, until last year." |
| **Their situation with uncomfortable precision** | "You quoted this job twice because the first gauge was wrong." |
| **A real concession before any claim** | "We're not the cheapest panel in Ontario. Here's the arithmetic anyway." |
| **A refused expectation** | Opening on the limitation, the price, or the disqualification |

What does not break it: bigger type, "revolutionary," "leading provider," stock heroes, exclamation marks, or a headline that could sit on a competitor's page unchanged. **The swap test:** put a competitor's name on your headline. If it still works, it has broken nothing.

### Authority — earn the credit, and collect the agentic shift

Authority does two jobs: it makes the claim believable, and via the agentic shift it *lowers the felt risk of acting*, which quietly dissolves objections you never addressed.

Authority in copy is carried by:

- **Precision.** Exact figures, named standards, real constraints. Vagueness reads as not knowing.
- **Willingness to state a limitation.** Only someone secure names a downside. This is the highest-yield and least-used authority move in marketing.
- **Command register.** Short declaratives. No hedging stack ("we believe we may be able to help").
- **Named humans with real credentials**, not a logo bar. A logo bar is tribe evidence, not authority — see below.
- **Specific methodology.** *How* you know, not just what you claim.

### Tribe — and the distinction people get wrong

Tribe is not "we have customers." Tribe is **"people like you, specifically, are already here."**

A logo bar of large recognizable companies signals authority-by-association. It does not signal tribe to a three-truck roofing contractor — it may actively signal *this is not for me*. Tribe evidence is the customer who looks like the reader: same size, same region, same trade, named.

Hughes' infomercial breakdown is exactly this: *everybody in your neighborhood's doing it.* Neighborhood, not Fortune 500.

Strongest to weakest: named customer who mirrors the reader → their words verbatim → count of people like them → generic logos → unattributed praise.

### Emotion — the rung that gets skipped

Almost every B2B page ends after Tribe. The reader agrees with everything and does nothing, because agreement is not action.

Emotion in a considered purchase is rarely excitement. It is usually one of:

- **The cost of the status quo, made concrete and personal.** Not "you may be losing business" but "the contractor who called and got voicemail bought from someone else, and he'll call them first next time too."
- **The paired fear, shown not stated** — a true story of someone who chose wrong, per the exposure play in [techniques.md](techniques.md).
- **Future pacing.** One or two sentences in the after-state, present tense, sensory: what the desk looks like when the spec question stops arriving by phone.
- **Relief.** For an overloaded buyer, the emotion that converts is the difficulty ending.

Show it in scenes, not adjectives. Hughes on the infomercial: *"they show it visually — they don't just say it."*

## The CTA — one small yes

The ask must be genuinely small, and *felt* to be small. A "free audit" behind twelve form fields is not a small yes, and it costs you every rung already climbed.

- Name the exact next action and its cost in time: "a 15-minute call, and you'll have the gap list whether or not we work together"
- Remove the unstated risk (what happens after they click, who calls, whether they get sold)
- Two options that both advance — a double bind — never one CTA and one exit
- Match the need: Power needs control ("pull the numbers yourself"), Approval needs reassurance ("no pitch"), Intelligence needs the substance up front

## The rule that makes all of it work

**These frameworks decide sequence, framing and emphasis. They never decide whether a claim is true.**

No invented statistics, no manufactured scarcity, no fabricated social proof, no "2 spots left." This is Geongyn's standing rule and it is also the reason the techniques work: a real finding survives being pushed on, and a manufactured one collapses the moment a buyer checks. Applied to true claims, this is very good marketing. Applied to false ones it is a liability with a shelf life.

The persuasion is in the *order* and the *framing*. The claims are just true.
