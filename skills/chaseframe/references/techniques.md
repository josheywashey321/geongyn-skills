# The technique library

The full linguistic and influence toolkit from the Hughes material. Nothing is withheld. Each entry gives the technique, the mechanism it runs on, and an example.

**Read the mechanism line.** Several of these run on *tonal shift* or *live timing* — that is not a warning, it is how the technique works. Knowing a tool's mechanism tells you what happens to it in a medium that lacks the mechanism. Use that knowledge to pick the right tool for the channel rather than deploying a voice technique into text and expecting voice results.

**Ranking, in his own words.** Hughes repeatedly puts these tools *below* the speaker: "there's no magic words" (C-JRE-1), scripts are "a placebo" (E-MN), "make your goal being a good communicator, not being good at tactics" (E-MN 0:22). The structures fail without composure, congruence and genuine perception — especially anything that names what someone feels.

**Tags added 2026-10-10.** Entries marked `[contested-science]` are NLP-derived; the research (see [evidence.md](evidence.md)) does not support the mechanism Hughes gives. Their *behavioral core* is often still useful and is stated in each entry. Codes like `A-B8L 12:24` resolve in evidence.md.

## Statement before question `[primary-spoken]`

A bare question reads as need and hands the other person the power to say no (A-VD1). Lead with a statement, then ask. His statement classes:

| Class | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alignment** | Something observably true they'd agree with | "Most owners your size are booked solid in January." |
| **Resonance** | Names what they're carrying before they do | "You've had the month where the trucks were full and the phone went quiet." |
| **Concession** | Gives something real away first | "Ads aren't the problem for most shops." |

Then a question type: **directional** (where to next), **assumptive** ("is this you, or the whole team?"), **reframe**, or **diagnostic**. If it misses: read the reaction, release it, switch statement class, redeploy (A-VD1). Choose by asking: what frame is running, and what do they need to do in the next 30 seconds?

With authority figures, only resonance or a concession gets you in (A-VD1).

## Make them feel clever (two Legos) `[primary-spoken]`

Place two true, familiar facts close together and never state the conclusion. The listener joins them and owns the idea: "any idea that you think came from your own mind, you have no ability to resist it" (D-DOAC-2 1:04). He calls it possibly the most dangerous persuasion skill there is, and uses it in trials.

**Everywhere.** Ads: Fact A, Fact B, end the ad. Pages: two data points side by side with no "therefore". Calls: show the result, show the comparison, stop talking. Both facts — and the conclusion they imply — must be true.

## Dissociation `[primary-spoken]`

Phrase the persuasive line as an observation about a type of person or about the world, never aimed at "you": "Does the unconscious filter by pronoun? Never" (A-B8L 12:24). **Negative dissociation** — a true observation about people the reader wants to step away from — is the strongest form (D-DOAC-2 0:22). It must sound like "an observation about the world".

**Works fully in text** — the decisive variable is the dissociation, not tone.

---

## "I imagine" statements and novel images `[primary-spoken]`

Certainty about someone is easy to refute; "I'd imagine you've…" isn't (E). Common metaphors are inert — they've been heard too often to register. Use unfamiliar, concrete images (E).

## Embedded commands `[secondary, structure per sobrief; primary confirmation of use]`

A directive hidden inside conversational speech, marked by a subtle change in volume, pitch, and pause.

**Three-part structure:**

| Part | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Vehicle** | Carries the command in without triggering screening | "A person can…" |
| **Command** | The actual directive | "…feel completely focused…" |
| **Continuum** | Returns to normal speech, closing the seam | "…when something interests them." |

The command words take a **slight downward tonal shift** — the unconscious reads a downward inflection as instruction rather than suggestion.

Hughes' own example: *"It's just really hard to focus right now"* — where **focus right now** is the command, said slightly louder.

**Mechanism (as he tells it):** tonal marking. `[contested-science]` — embedded commands are NLP-derived and the "goes straight to the subconscious" mechanism is not supported by research.
**In voice** — calls, video, voiceover: a pitch drop on the action line is still good delivery; it signals certainty.
**In text:** he later teaches the version that matters — the **dissociated** command: the directive phrased as an observation about other people ("some owners just check"), never aimed at the reader (A-B8L 12:24). That survives text completely; see Dissociation above. Bold or italic remains a weak substitute because visible emphasis is consciously processed.

## Presupposition `[secondary]`

Embed the thing you want accepted as an assumption inside a question about something else. The listener processes the surface question and swallows the assumption whole.

- *"When you start to feel a sense of trust with someone, what does it feel like?"* — presupposes the trust
- *"Did you notice the lights become dimmer?"* — presupposes the lights changed; asks only whether they noticed

**Mechanism:** attention is spent on the foreground question; the background proposition enters unexamined.
**Everywhere:** this is the tool that transfers most completely across every medium. In copy it is the difference between "if this is a fit" and "when you're deciding which tier fits."

**Identity presupposition** `[primary-spoken]`: "I would imagine you're the kind of person who…" (E-KC 0:14), or the triple presupposition "What was the moment you realized…" (A-ET0). Presuppose who they are, not just what they'll do. See [identity.md](identity.md).

## Double binds `[secondary]`

A choice where both options serve the same outcome.

- *"Do you feel more focused when you tune everything out, or when you collect all your attention in one place?"* — both answers accept focusing
- Commercially: *"Would Tuesday or Thursday work better?"* — both accept the meeting

**Mechanism:** shifts the decision from *whether* to *which*, and hands over a genuine, satisfying choice inside the frame you set.
**Everywhere.** In layout this is two CTAs that both advance (Get a Quote / Browse Specs), never one CTA and one exit.

**Tension at mass scale.** Hughes' own psyop checklist flags *false dilemmas* as a manipulation tell (E-HAN 0:09). In one-to-one conversation a bind is a courtesy; in mass copy aimed at sceptical buyers, an obvious bind can trip that detector. Keep binds where both options are genuinely useful to the buyer ("Comment SCAN, or DM me SCAN").

## Confusion into command `[primary-spoken; contested-science]`

*Not for use on anyone who hasn't agreed to the interaction — see the do-not-use list in [evidence.md](evidence.md). Kept so you recognise it.*

Hughes calls this a go-to he teaches for days at a time.

> "When you confuse a person, you can also throw an embedded command immediately after that and that goes directly to the subconscious part of the brain processing center."

> "With confusion, your brain is trying to make sense of what's going on. It's trying to process a statement that is kind of confusing, and it will accept the first thing that it hears."

Delivered as a deliberately nonsensical statement spoken with total conviction — *"Nobody knows what's going to happen a week ago isn't even the right place to start"* — immediately followed by a clear suggestion, then a return to normal conversation. He notes commands are "10 times more effective" mixed with confusion, and that the average person recalls under 20% of a conversation, so the confusion itself vanishes.

**Mechanism:** a processing stall creates a gap; the brain grabs the first coherent thing offered. Requires the subject to be *held* in the stall, which requires live delivery and conviction in the voice.
**In voice:** as described.
**In text:** a reader who stalls does not grab the next sentence — they leave, or re-read. The page has no way to hold them through the gap. What survives into text is the *pattern interrupt*: a line that breaks expected rhythm to reclaim attention, without the incoherence.

## Linguistic harvesting `[secondary]`

Collect the subject's own adjectives, sensory preferences, and pronoun patterns during casual conversation, then deploy those exact words back later.

If they call their neighbourhood "disgusting" and their home "calm" and "agreeable," those exact words become ammunition — the negatives for building aversion to the alternative, the positives for describing your outcome.

**Mechanism:** a person's own vocabulary carries their own emotional charge, pre-attached. It bypasses the translation step where your framing gets compared to theirs.
**In conversation:** harvest live.
**In marketing:** harvest from the source material — sales call recordings, review text, support tickets, the words in their RFQ. Then write the page in those words. This is the single highest-leverage technique in the library for copy, and it is just voice-of-customer research with a sharper reason for existing.

## Sensory preference matching `[secondary; contested-science]`

*NLP representational systems failed testing (Wiseman 2012). What survives is the voice-of-customer point at the end: use the trade's own words.*

People favour visual, auditory, or kinesthetic language. Match theirs.

- Visual: *see, clear, picture, looks like, focus*
- Auditory: *sounds right, tells me, ringing, clicks*
- Kinesthetic: *feel, solid, grasp, rough, hold up*

**Mechanism:** matching the channel reduces the translation cost of understanding you, which reads subjectively as rapport.
**In copy:** you cannot profile an individual reader, but trades skew kinesthetic and visual — *holds up, solid, you can see the seam* beats *sounds like a good option* to a roofer. Match the trade, not the person.

## Pacing and leading `[secondary]`

State several things that are unambiguously true and verifiable to the listener, then attach the thing you want them to accept. Agreement momentum carries across the seam.

*"You've got three crews out, the supplier moved your delivery twice, and you're quoting next season already — which is exactly why the spec data needs to be somewhere you can pull it up on your phone."*

**Mechanism, in his telling:** each *true* statement produces a small internal agreement below awareness, so the critical faculty never switches on — it's the truth of each statement that does the work, not a repetition groove (A-VD1; E-KC 0:14 adds an "I imagine" statement at the end of the run). **If a pace is rejected, don't pace harder — switch to a resonance statement** (A-VD1).
**Everywhere.** In copy this is the "problem" section: three sentences of their reality, then the pivot. The failure mode is pacing with something false — one wrong "true" statement and the whole chain inverts into resistance.

## Anchoring `[secondary, gesture example primary]`

Attach a state to a stimulus, then re-trigger the state by re-presenting the stimulus.

Hughes' spoken example: while describing being captivated, he touches his own chest — twice — so that the state of captivation associates with *him*.

**Mechanism:** classical association. Needs the state to actually be present when the anchor is set.
**In voice/video:** gesture, position, a recurring phrase.
**In copy and layout:** a repeated visual or verbal motif that appears at every emotional peak, then again at the CTA. A colour, a phrase, a mark. Weaker than live anchoring, and genuinely used in brand design.

## Fear-of-exposure plays `[primary example]`

Use the fear paired to the person's dominant need, rather than the need itself. Hughes' worked example, verbatim in substance:

A car-insurance buyer whose need is *intelligence*. You tell a story of someone who chose not to buy, had an accident, and found they were not covered — and you lament that they should have been more discerning. The buyer does not want to be the person in that story.

**Mechanism:** the buyer supplies the pressure themselves. Nothing was claimed about them, so there is nothing to argue with.
**Everywhere**, and it is the most potent tool here. Two operational notes: the story has to be true, and the fear has to be the one they actually run — an exposure play aimed at the wrong need lands as a non-sequitur and burns credibility.

## Elicitation `[primary-spoken]`

Getting information without asking questions. Statements invite correction; questions invite guarding. He credits the method to John Nolan, and says the more sensitive the topic, the fewer questions you ask (E). It works because it puts people in "friend mode".

- **Presumptive statement:** *"Most shops your size are still faxing takeoffs."* → they correct you with the real process
- **Deliberate error / correct the record:** name the wrong figure; people cannot resist fixing it (C)
- **Bracketing:** *"I'd guess a job like that runs thirty, forty thousand?"* → they narrow it
- **Provocative statement:** *"I bet…"* / *"So…"* followed by a summary they'll refine (E)
- **Disbelief:** *"No way that's working for you"* → they defend it with detail (C; E)
- **Projection:** *"How do most people in your spot react when…"* — they answer for themselves (A-nBn)
- **Quid pro quo:** disclose something first; reciprocity does the rest *(not mentioned by Hughes in the 2026 sources; standard practice)*

**Mechanism:** correction and reciprocity are lower-guard reflexes than answering.
**In conversation:** discovery calls, live chat.
**In marketing:** the form field that gets answered because a wrong default is pre-filled; the survey question phrased as a presumption.

## Attentional captivity and trance development `[secondary]`

Absorption deep enough that competing stimuli fade — the theatre effect. Named as the first operational goal of the Ellipsis Progression, and the foundation everything deeper is built on.

**Mechanism:** sustained, uninterrupted focus on a single channel, with the operator controlling pacing.
**In voice:** long-form, controlled tempo, no competing input.
**In marketing:** the closest real analogue is a long-form page or VSL that removes every exit — no nav, no sidebar, no competing CTA — and controls sequence absolutely. That is why long-form sales pages strip navigation. It is the same principle at low intensity.

## Breaking autopilot `[primary]`

The three levers, from [frameworks.md](frameworks.md): unusual speech, unusual behavior, authoritative presence.

**In marketing** the script being broken is *"marketing page, skim, leave."* What breaks it:

- A specific, checkable number where a vague claim belongs
- Saying the thing the category never says out loud
- Naming the reader's actual situation with more precision than a stranger should have
- Conceding something real before claiming anything
- A visual or structural pattern the category does not use

What never breaks it: bigger type, a stock hero image, an exclamation mark, or the word "revolutionary."

## The compliance ladder `[secondary]`

Small agreements make large ones easier. Sequence requests from trivial to significant, never opening with the large one.

**Mechanism:** consistency pressure — people act in line with what they have already done. Hughes adds an *identity* mechanism: the first act redefines who the person is (E), and the strongest rung is one the **buyer initiates** (B-1Ua).
**Everywhere.** In a funnel: read → scroll → micro-interaction → email → call → contract. Each rung must be genuinely small; a "small" ask that is actually large (a 12-field form) breaks the ladder and costs you the rungs already climbed.

**Gate every rung with the energy test** (A-Gz0): a yes that lowers their energy is compliance — it pauses resistance and dies when you leave the room. A yes that raises it is alignment. Stacked compliance teaches people to hide. See [identity.md](identity.md).
