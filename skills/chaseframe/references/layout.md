# Layout and page architecture

## Information architecture is not influence architecture

The default way to order a page is by topic: hero, trust bar, products, specs, gallery, about, FAQ, contact. Every section is defensible and the whole thing converts badly, because topic order optimizes for *finding* information. It assumes a reader who already wants the thing and is looking for a reason to say no.

FATE orders the same sections by **what state the reader has to be in to accept the next one.** A section placed before the reader can accept it is not neutral — it is spent. Proof shown before attention is earned is proof the reader never registered, and you do not get to show it again.

---

## The ladder as page structure

| Rung | Job | Sections that do it |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | Break the skim script; earn the next 10 seconds | Hero. Nothing else. |
| **Authority** | Establish who is talking and why they are credited | Methodology, precision, named humans, the honest limitation |
| **Tribe** | "People like me are already here" | Named customers who mirror the reader, their words, their region |
| **Emotion** | Convert agreement into motion | Cost of the status quo, the true cautionary story, the after-state |
| **Action** | The one small yes | CTA, de-risked, exact |

The ladder can repeat at lower amplitude on a long page. It cannot run out of order.

---

## Ordering rules

**1. The fold does one job: Focus.**
Not focus plus proof plus product plus a form. Split attention above the fold is the most common way a page fails at rung one and never recovers. Headline, one line of substantiation, one action.

**2. Authority before proof.**
Proof answers "is this true." Authority answers "should I be listening." A reader who has not credited you reads testimonials as marketing. Give them a reason to credit you first, and the same testimonials land as evidence.

**3. Tribe after authority, and make it mirror the reader.**
A logo bar of enterprise names is authority-by-association, not tribe. To a small buyer it can read *this is not for me*. Tribe is the customer their size, in their region, in their trade, named.

**4. Emotion late, and never skipped.**
It requires the earlier rungs to mean anything. This is the rung most B2B pages omit entirely, which is precisely why they get "great page, we'll think about it."

**5. Every section states its own conclusion.**
Do not make the reader infer. A section whose point survives only as a subtitle has not made it.

**6. The ask matches the altitude.**
Early asks are small, late asks can be larger. A demo request in the fold is asking for the top rung from a reader on the ground.

## Reference order — considered B2B purchase

Adjust to the actual business. The *order* is the load-bearing part.

1. **Hero — Focus.** Autopilot-break headline, one substantiating line, one primary action. No logo bar competing for the eye.
2. **The reality section — pacing.** Three sentences of their actual situation, verifiable to them, then the pivot. Buys agreement momentum and proves you know the work.
3. **Authority block.** How you know what you know: the methodology, the numbers, the standard you work to, the named humans. Include one real limitation — it does more for credibility than three more claims.
4. **The substance.** Product, spec, service — whatever the buyer actually evaluates. Positioned here because now it is being read by someone who is paying attention and crediting the source. On a manufacturer's site this is where extractable spec data lives.
5. **Tribe proof.** Named customers who look like the reader, in their words. Their vocabulary, not yours.
6. **Objection resolution.** The three real ones, answered plainly. Answering an objection before it is felt plants it; answering it after it forms and before the ask removes it.
7. **Cost of the status quo — Emotion.** Concrete, personal, true. What the current situation costs, in scenes.
8. **The path.** Three or four steps, so the yes has a visible shape and the unknown after the click disappears.
9. **CTA.** The small yes, de-risked, exact. Two options that both advance.
10. **FAQ.** Last objections, and the machine-readable surface that AI assistants quote.

## What breaks the ladder

| Mistake | What it costs |
|---|---|
| Logo bar above or beside the headline | Splits Focus; the hero now has two jobs and does neither |
| Testimonials before authority | Reads as marketing; the proof is spent unregistered |
| Price before authority | The number has nothing to be weighed against, so it is only ever "high" |
| Three audiences in one hero | Averages three needs into a voice that triggers none |
| Emotion missing | Full agreement, no movement |
| Every section the same weight and rhythm | No contrast, so nothing reads as important — the page becomes uniform texture and the skim script survives intact |
| Multiple competing CTAs at different altitudes | The reader picks the smallest, which is usually "leave" |

## Visual hierarchy as an attention instrument

The RAS wakes on **deviation**. Visual design either sustains that or squanders it.

- **Contrast is the budget.** If everything is emphasized, nothing deviates. One dominant element per viewport.
- **The eye goes to the odd one.** Reserve your strongest visual break for the rung that matters most on that screen — usually the headline, then the CTA.
- **Whitespace is an authority signal.** Crowding reads as urgency and neediness; space reads as composure. This is the cheapest authority move available in layout.
- **Faces pull attention and direct it.** A face looking at the CTA outperforms a face looking at the camera.
- **Motion outranks everything for attention and is usually a mistake** — it wakes the RAS for the animation, not the message, and spends the deviation budget on nothing.
- **Legibility is authority.** Cramped type, poor contrast, and content locked in images read as *unserious* before a word is processed — and for a spec buyer, unreadable data is the product failing in front of them.

## Email and sequence layout

The ladder compresses but survives.

- **Subject = Focus.** Same autopilot-break test. If a competitor could send it verbatim, it breaks nothing.
- **First line = Focus + the proof you looked.** Never a greeting. Specific observation only.
- **Middle = Authority + Tribe**, one or two sentences each. This is the whole body.
- **Second-to-last = Emotion.** One concrete line on the cost of the current situation.
- **Last = the small yes.** One ask, one sentence, low friction.

Across a multi-touch sequence, run the ladder as the arc: touch 1 Focus, touches 2–3 Authority and Tribe, touch 4 Emotion, touch 5 the ask. See [campaigns.md](campaigns.md).
