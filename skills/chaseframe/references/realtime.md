# Real-time behavioral reading

For live interaction — sales calls, discovery, meetings, in-person. This is where 6MX actually operates: a subject you can observe and adapt to in real time, which a page never gives you.

**What this file can and cannot do.** It can give you the observation targets, the sequence, and the call structure — genuinely usable on your next call. It cannot make you fluent at reading faces; micro-expression and cluster reading are perceptual skills built by repetition against feedback, the way a radiologist learns to read scans. Treat the profiling sections as a practice curriculum, and the call-structure sections as immediately usable.

**One hard limit (2026-10-10).** Reading deception from behavior is about 54% accurate — near chance (see [evidence.md](evidence.md)). Use observation for exactly one thing: **noticing a change from that person's own baseline, and asking a better question.** Never conclude someone is lying, and never let a "read" overrule what they actually tell you. Hughes' own order is change → clusters → context → likelihood (E-DRVN 0:53).

## The sequence

**Baseline → deviation → cluster → hypothesis → test.** Skipping to interpretation is the whole failure mode.

### 1. Baseline (first 60–90 seconds)

Before anything substantive, observe them in a relaxed state. Small talk is not filler — it is instrumentation.

Log their normal: posture and how they hold their hands · speaking pace and pitch · eye-contact pattern · gesture frequency · blink rate · breathing (chest or stomach) · filler words.

**Without a baseline, every observation is noise.** Crossed arms mean nothing. Crossed arms *from a person whose arms were open ninety seconds ago, closing the moment you named the price*, mean something.

### 2. Deviation

Watch for change, and note **what was happening at the moment it changed**. Timing carries the information; the gesture alone does not.

### 3. Cluster

One signal is noise. A cluster is several channels shifting together:

| Channel | Watch |
|---|---|
| **Verbal** | Word choice, pronouns, hedging, tense shifts, topic changes |
| **Paraverbal** | Pace, pitch, volume, pauses — leaks stress before the body does |
| **Kinesic** | Posture, gesture, feet, self-touch, barriers |
| **Micro-expression** | Fractional-second facial flashes |

**Contradiction between channels is the highest-information event.** Calm words at double the normal pace. "Sounds great" with a backward lean. The truth is in the mismatch.

### 4. Hypothesis, then test

A cluster gives you a hypothesis, not a conclusion. Test it: raise the topic again from a different angle and watch whether the cluster returns. A repeated cluster on the same subject is a finding; once is a coincidence.

## Reading the six needs live

You are listening for how they talk about **themselves**.

| Need | Verbal tells | Behavioral tells |
|---|---|---|
| **Significance** | Accomplishments, titles, name-drops, "I built / I run" | Status objects, takes the head of the table |
| **Approval** | Checks your reaction, softens positions, "does that make sense?" | Scans your face after speaking, laughs at their own lines |
| **Acceptance** | "We" language, group membership, consensus framing | Mirrors you quickly, avoids disagreement |
| **Intelligence** | Technical vocabulary, corrects small details, cites sources | Resists being sold to, asks how it works before what it costs |
| **Pity** | Recounts hardship, unfairness, "nobody understands what it takes" | Long problem narratives, waits for acknowledgment |
| **Strength/Power** | Volume, interruption, leadership stories, "I told them" | Controls agenda and pace, tests you early |

**One primary, one secondary** (his worksheet's PRI/SEC slots). Look for the pair — the combination is more useful than either alone. Significance + Strength is a very different call than Intelligence + Approval. He says needs show within about 90 seconds to two minutes of someone talking about themselves (C).

### Reading the decision style (six)

| Style | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| **Deviance** | "Nobody in our space does this — that's the point." |
| **Novelty** | "Is this new? Has anyone else done it yet?" |
| **Social** | "How will this look to the board / my crew / my customers?" |
| **Conformity** | "What's standard? What do others in the industry do?" |
| **Investment** | "What's the payback? What does this cost me over three years?" |
| **Necessity** | "Do I actually have to do this? What happens if I don't?" |

## Elicitation — getting information without interrogating

Questions raise guard. Statements invite correction.

| Move | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Presumptive statement** | State a plausible wrong thing | "Most shops your size are still faxing takeoffs." |
| **Deliberate error** | Name the wrong number | "You're running what, four crews?" |
| **Bracketing** | Offer a range | "A job like that runs thirty, forty?" |
| **Provocative statement** | "I bet…" / "So…" + a summary | "So the website's basically a brochure at this point." |
| **Disbelief** | Doubt a claim gently | "No way referrals are still carrying all of it." |
| **Quid pro quo** | Disclose first | Share a real constraint of yours; reciprocity does the rest |
| **Silence** | Stop talking after their answer | The most reliable tool here. People fill silence with the thing they were not going to say. |

Rule from Hughes (crediting John Nolan): the more sensitive the topic, the fewer questions you ask (E).

## Authority on a call

The five elements — **Confidence, Discipline, Leadership, Gratitude, Enjoyment** — are built off camera, not performed in a meeting ("who you are first, then what you say", C). What is available in the moment:

- **Look like you need nothing from the call.** The single biggest cue (A-B8L 02:08). Composure is what grants permission (B).
- **Explain less.** "The person who explains the most has the least power" (A). Explanation invites negotiation — state your price and your process, once.
- **Stillness.** Fidgeting is the fastest authority leak there is.
- **Pace.** Slower than feels natural. Rushing signals low status and low confidence.
- **Comfortable silence.** Hold a pause after a question. Whoever is uncomfortable first has less authority.
- **Precision.** Exact numbers, named standards. "Roughly" and "kind of" cost you a rung each.
- **One real limitation, stated early.** Only someone secure names a downside — highest-yield authority move available, and it disarms the objection at the same time.
- **Genuine interest.** Hughes' own entry point: "make them feel interesting, not interested… leadership through support."

The **agentic shift** is Hughes' explanation for why a credible expert gets fewer objections. `[contested-science]` — current research says people follow authority when they share its aim. So connect your authority to *their* goal out loud: it's the shared aim, not the title, that lowers felt risk.

## Discovery call structure

**Before the call — PCP.** Ask: in what context would booking the next step feel automatic? Build that context (the right prep, the right materials on screen) before you say anything (C; D).

**Opening (2 min) — frame and expectancy.** Their autopilot expects a pitch. If it's true, break it gently: "Let's do this a little bit differently — I'm not going to pitch you" (A-RbQ). Then a short **safety briefing** that is really the agenda: what you'll look at, how long, what they'll leave with (D). That fills expectancy. Genuine small talk is also baseline — you are instrumenting, not filling time.

**Their situation (10 min) — elicit and profile.** Mostly statements and silence. You are listening for the need pair, the filter, and their exact vocabulary. **Write their words down verbatim** — those words go into the proposal and the copy afterward. This is linguistic harvesting, and it is the highest-value output of the call.

**Your read (5 min) — authority.** Reflect what you found, precisely, including one thing that is not a problem. Naming a non-problem proves you are diagnosing rather than selling, and it is the moment credibility is actually established.

**The gap (5 min) — emotion, matched to their decision style.** The cost of the current situation, framed for how they justify decisions. Concrete, true, theirs. Two Legos work best here: show the result, show the comparison, stop talking (D-DOAC-2 1:04).

**The admission, without loss of face.** Owners rarely say "our marketing isn't working." Hughes' golden bridge makes it easy (C-SRS 1:29; E-MN 0:46): *socialize* ("most owners your size are in the same spot"), *minimize* ("it's a small fix compared with what you've already built"), *rationalize* ("nobody shows owners this channel"), *project* ("this isn't something you missed — it's new"), then an either/or question ("is it the website or the follow-up that's leaking?").

**The ask (2 min) — one small yes.** Specific, low friction, easy to decline. Then watch the **energy test**: does their energy rise after the yes, or drop? A dropping yes is a polite exit — ask what would make it a real yes (A-Gz0). The "magic wand" question ("if you could change one thing about how jobs come in, what would it be?") surfaces the real need (D).

**Qualifying signals he uses** (E): how they talk about catching a cold (internal vs external locus of control); "what's the biggest lesson you've learned about yourself?"; asking them to rate their own candour 1–10.

## Conflict and resistance

- **"What makes you say that?"** — the neutral first response to pushback (A-Yvy). It buys time and gets the real objection.
- **Common ground, then shrink the issue.** Separate the idea from their identity; disagree with the idea, never the person (E).
- **Concession before any hard ask** (A-VD1). With a high-status buyer, resonance or a real concession is the only way in.
- **Never** argue with a stated identity ("we've always done referrals") — position alongside it.

## Objection handling by need

The same objection means different things depending on who says it.

| Objection | Need underneath | Response |
|---|---|---|
| "Too expensive" from **Power** | Testing whether you fold | Hold the number. Flexing confirms it was inflated. |
| "Too expensive" from **Investment filter** | Genuine arithmetic | Show the math over time. They want to believe it. |
| "Need to think about it" from **Approval** | Fears making a wrong call alone | Reassure that the choice is normal; offer a smaller step |
| "Need to think about it" from **Intelligence** | Insufficient data to defend the decision | Send the substance. Do not follow up with pressure — it insults the need. |
| "We already have someone" from **Acceptance** | Loyalty is identity | Do not attack the incumbent. Position alongside. |
| "Send me information" from **anyone** | Usually a soft exit | Ask what specifically would make it a yes. The answer tells you the real rung that is missing. |

## The Behavioral Table of Elements

`[primary-written; contested-science]` — the deception ratings are not validated (see [evidence.md](evidence.md)). 120+ behaviors, deception-rated 1.0–4.0, plotted head-to-feet vertically and low-stress-to-high-deception horizontally, with roughly 14 data points per cell including confirming gestures, amplifying gestures, cultural prevalence, and the window in which the behavior is diagnostic relative to a statement.

Using the full table properly requires the source material and sustained practice. The portion that transfers immediately is the principle already stated above: **rate clusters, not gestures, and always against that person's baseline.** A deception rating applied to an isolated signal, with no baseline, is worse than no reading at all — it produces confident wrong conclusions about people, and in a sales context that means walking away from a real buyer because they touched their face.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Why it costs you |
|---|---|
| Reading without a baseline | Every conclusion is noise wearing a lab coat |
| Single signals | Clusters carry meaning; gestures do not |
| Confirmation bias | Go in believing they are lying and you will find evidence. Observe with curiosity or do not observe. |
| Mimicking instead of matching | Copying gestures in real time is noticed and is creepy. Match pace and energy, 10–15 seconds behind. |
| Ignoring the paraverbal channel | Pace and pitch leak first, and are easiest to hear |
| Profiling instead of listening | Running the framework so hard you miss what they said. The framework serves the conversation, not the reverse. |
| Concluding someone is lying | Behavioral lie detection is near chance. A change is a reason to ask, never a verdict |
| Over-explaining | Every extra justification reads as need and invites negotiation |
| Treating a yes as commitment | Check the energy after it; a flat yes is a polite exit |
